Naked pair

Two cells in a unit share the same two candidates, so no other cell in the unit can use them. Level: Hard.

A naked pair is two cells in the same row, column or box whose notes show exactly the same two digits and nothing else, for example 6 and 8 in both. You do not know which cell is the 6 and which is the 8, but you do know that those two cells will use up both digits between them. So no other cell in that unit can be a 6 or an 8.

The pair is "naked" because the two digits sit in the two cells without any other candidates around them. If the two cells also share a second unit (two cells in one row are often in one box as well), the pair cleans both units.

How to spot it

  1. Fill in pencil marks for the area you are working on.
  2. Look for cells with exactly two candidates. These are called bivalue cells.
  3. When you find one, look along its row, its column and its box for a second cell with the same two candidates.
  4. Remove both digits from every other cell of the unit the two cells share.
  5. If the two cells share a row and a box, or a column and a box, do it for both.

A worked example

The two amber cells hold the same two candidates and nothing more. In their shared unit, the red copies of those digits are removed from the other cells.

Why it works

Imagine that some third cell in the unit took the 6. The pair would then have only the 8 left for two cells, and one of them would be empty for ever. The same happens if a third cell takes the 8. So neither digit can live outside the pair.

Another example

In the second position the pair sits in a different kind of unit. Check whether the two cells also share a second unit: if they do, look there for more removals.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Treating two cells as a pair when one of them has a third candidate. For a naked pair both cells must show exactly two candidates, the same two. (Two digits confined to two cells that also hold other candidates is a hidden pair, a different pattern.)
  • Removing the digits from the pair itself. The pair keeps its notes. Only the other cells of the unit lose them.
  • Using a pair across units. Both cells must be in the same row, the same column or the same box. Two matching cells that do not see each other prove nothing.

When to look for it

Naked pairs are common in Hard puzzles, and they are easy to see once notes are written, since matching two-digit cells catch the eye. The "Fill notes" button on our board writes all candidates for you; after that, scan each unit for twins.

Practise it

Play a puzzle at the Hard level, where this technique is needed. The first tap on Hint tells you when it is the step to look for.
