Cross-hatching: scanning one digit at a time

Cross-hatching is not a separate rule of logic. It is a way of looking, and it is how experienced solvers find hidden singles at speed without writing a single note. If you learn one habit from this site, make it this one.

The idea

Take one digit, for example 5. Every 5 already in the grid "shades" its whole row and its whole column: no other 5 can go there. Now look at a box that has no 5 yet. Some of its cells are filled, some are shaded by 5s in the crossing rows, some by 5s in the crossing columns. If exactly one empty cell is left unshaded, that cell is the 5.

The name comes from the picture this makes if you draw the shading on paper: horizontal and vertical strokes crossing the box, like hatching in a pencil drawing.

Step by step

  1. Pick the digit that appears most. Count the clues quickly. A digit that is already placed five or six times leaves only three or four boxes to check, and each is heavily shaded.
  2. Work in bands. The three boxes side by side form a band; the three boxes one above another form a stack. If two boxes of a band contain the digit, in two different rows, the third box must take it in the remaining row. That leaves at most three cells, and filled cells and column shading usually reduce them to one.
  3. Shade with columns too. For the target box, look up and down the three columns that cross it. Each column that already has the digit removes its cells.
  4. Place or park. One open cell: write the digit. Two open cells in a line: remember them (they may be a pointing pair). Three or more: move on.
  5. Go through all nine digits, then start again. The second round finds cells that the first round opened.

A worked band

Suppose the top band of a puzzle looks like this, with dots for empty cells:

. . 3 | . 7 . | . . .
7 . . | . . . | . 4 .
. . . | . . 2 | . . 6

Follow the 7. The left box has it in the middle row; the centre box has it in the top row. The right box needs a 7, and the top and middle rows are closed. Its 7 must be in the bottom row: one of the first two cells, since the third holds a 6. If a 7 already stands lower down in one of those two columns, you have your answer. If not, you know the 7 is one of two cells, and you look at the next digit.

Why it is so effective

A person finds it hard to answer "which of nine digits fits this cell?", because that needs up to twenty cells to be checked. It is much easier to answer "where is the gap for this digit?", because the eye follows straight lines well. Cross-hatching turns the puzzle into the second kind of question. Our Easy puzzles are built so that this scan, plus the occasional naked single, is enough from start to finish.

Tips

  • Use a finger or the end of a pen to trace rows on paper. On our board, tap any 5 and every 5 is highlighted, which makes the shading easy to see.
  • Do not stop a sweep at the neighbouring box. A digit at the far end of a row shades the row just as well.
  • When boxes give nothing, cross-hatch rows and columns: for a row with three gaps, check which of its missing digits are blocked by columns and boxes.
  • When a digit is placed nine times, forget it. The number pad on our board shows how many of each digit are left.

When scanning runs out

In Hard and Expert puzzles a full round of cross-hatching will sooner or later find nothing. That is the moment to write pencil marks and look for pairs and locked candidates. After each of those removes a candidate, go back to scanning: the easy moves return.
