Sudoku solving techniques

Eleven techniques in the order you should learn them, each with worked examples from real puzzles, plus the two skills underneath them all.

Every sudoku, however hard, is solved by repeating two kinds of step. A placement writes a digit in a cell because nothing else is possible. An elimination removes a candidate from a cell because logic rules it out, which sooner or later leads to a placement. The two singles below are the only placements; everything else on the list is an elimination. That is why the advanced techniques never feel like a finish: they clear the way and a single does the rest.

Learn them in order. Each one is rarer and slower to find than the one before, so a good solver always tries them from the top. Our puzzle levels are graded on the same ladder: the level of a puzzle is the highest step it forces you to take. The grading page explains this in full.

Start here: two basic skills

  • Cross-hatching
    Scan one digit along rows and columns to find its only free cell in a box.
  • Pencil marks
    Write candidates as notes, and keep them correct.

Singles: Easy and Medium puzzles

These two place digits. Together they solve every Easy and Medium puzzle on this site, and they finish every harder puzzle once the way is clear.

  • Naked single
    A cell where only one digit is still possible.
  • Hidden single
    A digit that has only one possible cell in a row, column or box.

Locked candidates and pairs: Hard puzzles

The first eliminations. They work inside one box and one line, or inside one unit, so they are quick to check once notes are written.

  • Pointing pairs
    In a box, a digit is limited to one row or column, so it leaves the rest of that line.
  • Box-line reduction
    In a row or column, a digit is limited to one box, so it leaves the rest of that box.
  • Naked pair
    Two cells in a unit share the same two candidates, so no other cell in the unit can use them.
  • Hidden pair
    Two digits fit in only the same two cells of a unit, so those cells can hold nothing else.

Triples, fish and wings: Expert puzzles

Larger patterns that reach across the grid. An Expert puzzle here usually needs one or two of them among many simpler steps.

  • Naked triple
    Three cells in a unit hold only three digits between them, so those digits leave the rest of the unit.
  • Hidden triple
    Three digits fit in only the same three cells of a unit, so those cells drop all other notes.
  • X-Wing
    A digit has two possible cells in each of two rows, in the same two columns, so it leaves the rest of those columns.
  • XY-Wing
    A pivot cell with two candidates and two wing cells remove a shared digit from the cells that see both wings.
  • Swordfish
    An X-Wing grown to three rows and three columns.

How to study a technique

  1. Read the idea and the "why it works" part until you could explain it to someone else.
  2. Cover the caption of the worked example and try to find the pattern in the picture.
  3. Play a puzzle at the matching level. When you are stuck, tap Hint once: it names the technique and the unit, and leaves the finding to you.
  4. Use the solver on puzzles from elsewhere to see which techniques they needed.

What is not on the list

Beyond these lie chains, colouring, unique rectangles and other methods used for the hardest published puzzles. We leave them out on purpose: every puzzle made here is solvable with the eleven above, and we teach only what our own solver can explain step by step. New words on the way? The glossary has them all, and the solving routine shows how the techniques fit together in a real game.
