Pointing pairs

In a box, a digit is limited to one row or column, so it leaves the rest of that line. Level: Hard.

Sometimes you cannot yet say which cell of a box takes a digit, but you can say which line it will be on. Suppose the only cells of a box that can still hold a 4 are two cells side by side in the same row. One of them will be the 4. Either way, that row gets its 4 inside this box, so no other cell of the row, outside the box, can be a 4.

The two (or three) cells "point" along the line like an arrow, and the digit is wiped from the cells they point at. This is the first technique that removes candidates instead of placing a digit. It is also called locked candidates, type 1, or an intersection removal.

How to spot it

  1. Work with pencil marks. Take one box and one digit that the box still needs.
  2. Find every cell of the box where the digit is still a candidate.
  3. If there are two or three such cells and they all lie in one row (or all in one column), you have a pointing pair or triple.
  4. Follow that row or column out of the box and remove the digit from every other cell on it.
  5. Check what the removal has done: often a cell on the line drops to one candidate, or the digit becomes a hidden single in another box.

A worked example

This position comes from a real puzzle made by this site. Rows are counted from the top and columns from the left, so R2C9 means row 2, column 9. Small digits are candidates.

The amber cells are the only places for the digit inside their box, and they share a line. The red candidates on the same line, outside the box, are removed.

Why it works

The box must contain the digit, and all its possible places in the box are on one line. So the line receives the digit from this box. A line can hold a digit only once, so the copy of the digit elsewhere on the line is impossible.

Another example

A second position with the pair lying the other way. Notice that nothing is placed yet: the gain is that the red candidates disappear, which opens the next single.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Using it when the digit has a third possible cell in the box, off the line. Every candidate of that digit in the box must be on the line. One stray candidate and the pattern is not there.
  • Removing the digit from the pointing cells themselves. The pair stays. Only the cells outside the box lose the digit.
  • Removing the digit from the rest of the box. That is the reverse technique, box-line reduction, and it needs the reverse condition.

When to look for it

Look for pointing pairs as soon as singles dry up. They are the cheapest pattern to find, because you only compare notes inside one box. Many people spot them without full pencil marks: while cross-hatching a digit, they notice that it is squeezed into one row of a box and continue the sweep along that row.

Practise it

Play a puzzle at the Hard level, where this technique is needed. The first tap on Hint tells you when it is the step to look for.
