Pencil marks: how to use notes

Pencil marks are small digits written in an empty cell to record what could still go there. They are your memory on paper. Easy puzzles do not need them. From Hard upwards, every technique works on them, so writing them correctly is a skill of its own.

What a candidate is

A candidate for a cell is any digit that does not already appear in the cell's row, column or box. At the start, a cell in a crowded corner may have two candidates, and a cell in an empty area six or seven. As the grid fills, candidates fall away, and when a cell is down to one you have a naked single.

Two styles of notes

  • Full notation. Every empty cell shows every candidate. Each digit has a fixed position in the cell: 1 2 3 on the top line, 4 5 6 in the middle, 7 8 9 at the bottom. Because the positions are fixed, patterns jump out: two cells with marks in the same two corners are a naked pair. This is the style our board uses.
  • Corner marks for pairs only. Many paper solvers write a digit small in a corner only when it has just two possible cells in a box. This keeps the grid clean and still shows pointing pairs at a glance. Its weakness is that naked singles and wings are harder to see.

When to start writing

Do not fill in notes at the first move. Notes in a nearly empty grid are long, slow to write and of little use. First take everything that cross-hatching gives. Start notes when a full round of scanning finds nothing. By then most cells have two to four candidates, and the notes are short enough to read.

How to write them without mistakes

  1. Go box by box. For each box, list the digits it is missing.
  2. For each empty cell of the box, strike from that list every digit found in the cell's row and column. Write what remains.
  3. When you place a digit anywhere, at once remove it from the notes of every cell in the same row, column and box. Forgetting this is the commonest cause of a ruined puzzle.
  4. When a technique removes a candidate, rub it out properly. A half-erased note will mislead you later.

Notes on this site's board

  • Tap Notes to switch note mode on, select a cell and tap digits to add or remove them. On a keyboard, press N.
  • Fill notes writes every legal candidate into every empty cell in one tap. It does only the simple elimination by row, column and box; it does not apply any technique for you.
  • When you place a digit, the board removes it from the notes of the cells that see it.
  • Some players think Fill notes is a shortcut too far. That is a matter of taste. Writing notes by hand teaches you the grid; filling them by machine lets you practise pattern-spotting. Try both.

Reading the notes

Once the notes are in, look for these shapes, in this order:

  • a cell with one mark: a naked single;
  • a digit that appears once in a box, row or column: a hidden single;
  • a digit whose marks in a box lie on one line: a pointing pair;
  • two cells in a unit with the same two marks: a naked pair;
  • anything longer: see the full list of techniques.

A warning about guess marks

Never write a note for a digit you hope is right. A note states a fact: this digit is still possible here. If you want to test a guess, use a different colour on paper, or take a note of the position, so that you can go back cleanly.
