X-Wing

A digit has two possible cells in each of two rows, in the same two columns, so it leaves the rest of those columns. Level: Expert.

The X-Wing is the first technique that looks at the whole grid for a single digit. Pick a digit, say 6. Suppose that in row 1 the 6 can go in only two cells, and in row 7 it can also go in only two cells, and these four cells line up in the same two columns, forming the corners of a rectangle.

Row 1 puts its 6 in one corner; row 7 must then put its 6 in the opposite corner, because the first column is taken. The two 6s sit on a diagonal of the rectangle, one way or the other, which is the X in the name. Both ways, each of the two columns receives a 6 from these rows. So every other 6 candidate in those two columns can be removed.

How to spot it

  1. Choose one digit and look only at its candidates. (On paper, some solvers lightly ring that digit across the grid.)
  2. Find a row where the digit has exactly two possible cells. Note their columns.
  3. Find another row where the digit has exactly two possible cells in the same two columns.
  4. Remove the digit from all other cells of those two columns.
  5. Then try the same with columns as the base: two columns, two cells each, in the same two rows, and clean the rows.

A worked example

This position comes from a real puzzle made by this site. Rows are counted from the top and columns from the left, so R2C9 means row 2, column 9. Small digits are candidates.

The four amber cells are the corners. In each of the two base lines the digit has no other home. The red candidates in the crossing lines are removed.

Why it works

Each of the two rows needs the digit, and has just the two corner cells for it. If the top row uses the left corner, the bottom row cannot use the left column and takes the right corner; if the top row uses the right corner, the bottom row takes the left. In both cases the left column and the right column each get the digit from one of the corners. A column holds a digit once, so the digit cannot stand anywhere else in either column.

Another example

In the second position the base lines run the other way. Whichever way the rectangle is built, removals happen in the lines that cross the base lines, never in the base lines themselves.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Using rows where the digit has three possible cells. Each base row must have exactly two candidates for the digit, both on the rectangle.
  • Removing candidates from the base rows. There is nothing to remove there. The removals are in the two columns (or in the rows, when columns are the base).
  • Seeing a rectangle of candidates that is not confined. Four candidates at the corners of a rectangle are not enough; the two base lines must have no other cell for that digit.

When to look for it

Reach for the X-Wing when pairs, triples and box-line work have all been tried. Digits that are already placed four or five times are the best to study, since their remaining candidates are few and patterns among them are easy to see.
