Swordfish

An X-Wing grown to three rows and three columns. Level: Expert.

The Swordfish is the X-Wing one size up. Take one digit. Find three rows in which that digit can go in only two or three cells each, and check the columns of all those cells. If, across the three rows, the candidates fall in just three columns in total, you have a Swordfish.

The three rows need three copies of the digit, one each, and the only columns on offer are those three. So each of the three columns gets its copy from one of these rows, and the digit can be removed from every other cell of the three columns. As with naked triples, a row does not need a candidate in all three columns; two is enough.

How to spot it

  1. Choose a digit with plenty of candidates left but no placement in sight.
  2. List, for each row, the columns where the digit is still possible. Keep rows with two or three such columns.
  3. Look for three rows whose lists together name only three columns.
  4. Remove the digit from the other cells of those three columns.
  5. Repeat with columns as the base and rows as the cleaning lines.

A worked example

This position comes from a real puzzle made by this site. Rows are counted from the top and columns from the left, so R2C9 means row 2, column 9. Small digits are candidates.

The amber cells are every candidate of the digit in the three base lines. They occupy only three crossing lines. The red candidates on those crossing lines are removed.

Why it works

Three rows must each place the digit once, in different columns, and all their options lie within three columns. Three placements in three columns means every one of those columns is used by one of the three rows. No other row can then place the digit in those columns.

Another example

In the second position the fish lies the other way. Count the amber cells in each base line: two or three, never more, and always within the same three crossing lines.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Expecting a full three by three block of nine candidates. Most Swordfish are incomplete, with six or seven candidates. Only the three-columns-in-total test matters.
  • Including a row with a candidate in a fourth column. One stray candidate in a base row breaks the pattern.
  • Hunting for it before simpler things. A Swordfish takes time to verify. Make sure no X-Wing, wing or triple is available first.

When to look for it

Swordfish are uncommon; an Expert puzzle on this site needs one only now and then. A good sign is a digit that has two or three candidates in most rows and no hidden single anywhere. If you enjoy the idea, the same logic with four rows and four columns exists too, and is called a Jellyfish, but our puzzles never need it.

Practise it

Play a puzzle at the Expert level, where this technique is needed. The first tap on Hint tells you when it is the step to look for.
