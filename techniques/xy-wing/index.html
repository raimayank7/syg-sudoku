XY-Wing

A pivot cell with two candidates and two wing cells remove a shared digit from the cells that see both wings. Level: Expert.

The XY-Wing uses three cells that each have exactly two candidates. The middle cell, the pivot, holds X and Y. One wing shares a unit with the pivot and holds X and Z. The other wing shares a different unit with the pivot and holds Y and Z. The two wings need not see each other.

Now follow the pivot. If the pivot is X, the first wing cannot be X and becomes Z. If the pivot is Y, the second wing becomes Z. So one of the two wings is certainly Z. Any cell that sees both wings can never be Z.

How to spot it

  1. Look for a cell with two candidates; call them X and Y. This is your pivot.
  2. Among the cells that share a row, column or box with the pivot, look for one with candidates X and Z, where Z is a new digit.
  3. Look for another cell that the pivot sees, with candidates Y and Z.
  4. Find the cells that see both wings (share a unit with each of them), leaving out the pivot.
  5. Remove Z from those cells.

A worked example

This position comes from a real puzzle made by this site. Rows are counted from the top and columns from the left, so R2C9 means row 2, column 9. Small digits are candidates.

The caption names the pivot first, then the two wings. Trace both cases with your finger: each value of the pivot pushes one wing to the shared digit, which is why the red candidate cannot stay.

Why it works

The pivot has only two possible values. Each value forces one wing to become Z. Since one of the two cases must happen, at least one wing is Z, though you cannot yet say which. A cell that shares a unit with both wings would clash with whichever wing turns out to be Z.

Another example

A second position. The wings often lie in different boxes, and the cells they both see can be few, sometimes only one. One removal is enough if it opens a single.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing wings that the pivot does not see. Each wing must share a row, column or box with the pivot.
  • Using cells with three candidates. All three cells must be bivalue for the basic XY-Wing.
  • Removing Z from cells that see only one wing. The target must see both wings.
  • Removing X or Y. Only the digit that the two wings have in common, and the pivot lacks, is removed.

When to look for it

When a stuck grid has many two-candidate cells, an XY-Wing is likely. Scan the bivalue cells and look for three that share digits in a chain: XY, XZ, YZ. On this site, Expert puzzles often need one.

Practise it

Play a puzzle at the Expert level, where this technique is needed. The first tap on Hint tells you when it is the step to look for.
