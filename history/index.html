The history of sudoku, and some numbers

Sudoku looks ancient and is not. The puzzle in its present form is less than fifty years old, was born in the United States, was named in Japan, and reached the rest of the world through a retired judge and a London newspaper. The dates and figures on this page were checked against the Wikipedia articles "Sudoku" and "Mathematics of Sudoku" on 12 November 2026.

1979: Number Place

The modern puzzle first appeared in 1979 in a puzzle magazine of the American publisher Dell Magazines, under the name Number Place. It carried no author's name. It is now thought to be the work of Howard Garns, a retired architect from Indiana, then 74 years old: a later search of the magazines found his name in the list of contributors only in the issues that contained Number Place. Garns died in 1989, before his puzzle became famous.

1984: Japan gives it a name

In April 1984 the Japanese puzzle company Nikoli, led by Maki Kaji, printed the puzzle in its paper Monthly Nikolist with a long title that means roughly "the digits must be single". The title was soon shortened to its first characters: su, number, and doku, single. That is the word sudoku. In Japan the name is a trademark of Nikoli, so other Japanese publishers often use Number Place, or the short form Nanpure.

1997 to 2004: a program and a newspaper

In 1997 Wayne Gould, a judge who had retired in Hong Kong, saw a sudoku book in a Tokyo bookshop. He spent the next six years writing a computer program that could produce puzzles and rate their difficulty. A small American newspaper, The Conway Daily Sun of New Hampshire, printed one of his puzzles in September 2004. Then The Times of London began a daily puzzle on 12 November 2004, calling it Su Doku. Other newspapers followed within months, and by 2005 sudoku was a worldwide habit.

Competitions

The World Puzzle Federation has organised a World Sudoku Championship every year since 2006, except 2020 and 2021.

A special kind of Latin square

A square grid in which every row and every column holds each symbol once is called a Latin square, an idea studied by mathematicians long before sudoku. A completed sudoku is a Latin square with one more condition: the boxes. That extra condition is what makes the puzzle solvable from few clues.

The first figure is so large that if every person on Earth solved a new grid every second, they would not come to the end of the list in many thousands of years. This is why a generator, such as the one on this site, can go on making fresh puzzles and will, in practice, never show you the same one twice by chance.

Why at least eight digits must appear among the clues

Here is a small proof you can check yourself. Suppose a puzzle's clues used only seven of the nine digits, with no 8 and no 9 anywhere. Take its solution and swap every 8 with every 9. The result still obeys the rule and still matches all the clues. So the puzzle would have two solutions. A proper puzzle must therefore show at least eight different digits.

How puzzles are made today

Nikoli is known for puzzles composed by hand. Most others, including ours, are made by program: fill a grid at random, then remove clues one or two at a time, checking after each removal that the solution is still the only one. Our generator removes clues in pairs placed opposite each other through the centre, a custom that gives printed sudoku their balanced look. The grading comes afterwards.
