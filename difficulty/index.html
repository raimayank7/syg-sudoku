Sudoku difficulty levels explained

Two puzzles with the same number of clues can be worlds apart: one falls in five minutes, the other holds out for an hour. Difficulty does not come from how many digits are given. It comes from what kind of reasoning the next step needs. This page explains how we grade the puzzles made on this site, so that you know what a level asks of you.

How a puzzle is graded here

After the generator makes a puzzle and confirms it has one solution, a second program solves it the way a person would: step by step, always trying the simplest technique first. The hardest technique it was forced to use sets the level. If the puzzle cannot be finished with the techniques taught on this site, it is thrown away and another is made. So every puzzle here can be solved by logic, and the Hint button can always explain the next step.

The clue counts are what our generator produced in test runs in November 2026; a single puzzle may fall a little outside them.

Why clue count is a poor guide

Look at the table: Medium, Hard and Expert overlap almost fully in clue count. Our generator removes clues in mirror pairs until no more can go without creating a second solution, whatever the level. Whether the result needs an X-Wing or only singles depends on where the remaining clues sit, not on how many there are. Only Easy is different: there the generator stops early, at about 38 clues, which leaves many moves open at every moment.

The smallest number of clues any proper 9 by 9 sudoku can have is 17. That was proved by an exhaustive computer search published in 2014. Such puzzles are rare, and they are not always hard.

What "hard" feels like

  • Width. In an easy puzzle there may be ten moves available at once; miss one and you find another. In a hard one there may be a single way forward. This narrowness, more than any named technique, is what makes a puzzle slow.
  • Depth. Some steps place nothing. A pointing pair only removes a candidate; the reward comes one move later. Puzzles with several such steps in a row need you to trust the process.
  • Notes. A Hard puzzle is hard partly because it needs accurate pencil marks, and keeping them accurate is work.

Other sites and newspapers grade differently

There is no standard. One newspaper's "fiendish" is another's "medium", and names such as gentle, tough, diabolical and evil mean whatever the editor decides. Some graders count how many moves are open on average; some add points for every technique used. If you move between sources, give yourself two or three puzzles to learn what their labels mean.

Beyond Expert

There are puzzles that none of our eleven techniques can finish. They need longer chains of reasoning with names such as colouring, forcing chains and unique rectangles, or plain trial and error. We do not serve them, because we could not explain every step. If you meet one elsewhere, our solver will still find its answer and tell you how far the basic techniques go.

Choosing your level

If you finish a level in a comfortable time without hints three times in a row, move up. If a level takes more than twice your usual time, you are learning; stay there. Start here: Easy, Medium, Hard, Expert.
