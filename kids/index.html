Sudoku for children: start small

A full sudoku has 81 cells, which is a lot for a young child to hold in mind. The mini grids on this page use the same single rule on a smaller square. The 4 by 4 grid uses the digits 1 to 4 and has four boxes of two by two. The 6 by 6 grid uses 1 to 6 and has six boxes, each two rows high and three columns wide. Pick a size with the buttons above the board.

The rule, in a child's words

"Every row needs each number once. Every column needs each number once. Every thick-lined box needs each number once. No number may sit twice in the same line or the same box."

How to teach it in ten minutes

  1. Show a finished 4 by 4 grid first. Let the child check a row, a column and a box and see that each has 1, 2, 3 and 4.
  2. Start with one empty cell. Point to a row with three numbers and ask, "Which number is missing?" Children enjoy being right at once.
  3. Then two empty cells in a row. "It is 2 or 3. Look up and down: is there already a 2 in this column?" This is the whole method of sudoku in one question.
  4. Let them tap. On this board the child taps the cell, then the number. A number that breaks the rule turns red, so mistakes show themselves without an adult saying "wrong".
  5. Praise the reason, not the speed. Ask "How do you know?" after a correct number. Saying the reason aloud is the real skill.

Which size for which age?

As a rough guide from the way the puzzles work, not a rule: a child who can count to four and recognise the digits can try the 4 by 4 grid with an adult beside them. The 6 by 6 grid needs a little more patience, since some cells cannot be filled until others are done. When a child finishes 6 by 6 grids alone, the Easy 9 by 9 is a fair next step. Every child is different; let interest lead.

Play on paper too

The print page makes 4 by 4 and 6 by 6 puzzles, up to six on a sheet, with an answer page. Paper is good for young children: a pencil, an eraser and no screen. For a first try, you can also draw a 4 by 4 grid by hand and use four coloured counters or four small toys in place of numbers. The puzzle works with any four different things.

What children gain

We make no brain-training claims. What a mini sudoku plainly practises is useful in itself: looking along a line, holding a rule in mind, checking before writing, and staying with a small problem until it is finished.

For the classroom

  • Put one 4 by 4 puzzle on the board and solve it together, a cell at a time, asking a different child for each reason.
  • Give pairs of children one printed 6 by 6 puzzle between them, so that they must explain their moves to each other.
  • Keep a few printed sheets ready for children who finish other work early.
